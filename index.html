The idea

It doesn't cut on your line

The built-in chamfer toolpaths fight you. You end up guessing at depths, and the very tip of the V-bit does the cutting — the smallest, weakest part of the cutter, and the part that leaves the worst finish.

EdgeBreaker takes a different route. It draws a new line out in the waste, offset from the one you selected, and cuts that line with Machine Vectors set to On. The side of the flute forms the chamfer. The tip and its flat run beside the wall in clear air, never dragging through your cut.

That's the whole trick, and everything else follows from it.

The standoff, and the cut position

How far out is the interesting number

That offset is the standoff, and it's the one number you're really choosing. The drawing at the top of this page is it, live — labelled G there, and in the gadget's after-run report.

Push the cut further out into the waste and the V-bit has to go deeper before its slanted face reaches your edge. Same chamfer, deeper plunge, and a higher spot on the flute doing the work.

So you pick a position from 0 to 100 up the flute, by dragging the blue dot on the bit. 0 is the smallest gap — the cut sits low, just clear of the tip. 100 is the largest, up near the top of the flute. Cutting high uses fresh, wide cutting edge instead of a worn point. You pay for it in depth, and the whole drawing redraws as you drag, so you see what it costs before you commit.

Both ends of the range are there to keep the cut off the two spots a V-bit cuts badly: the tip, and the shoulder where the flute runs out. A chamfer eats into the top of that range, so past a certain size there's nothing left in between and the bit can't take it in one go. So it takes it in several.

Big chamfers

More chamfer than the bit can take

Ask for a chamfer bigger than the bit manages in one bite and it doesn't get turned down. EdgeBreaker works out how many passes that takes and builds them all. The dialog says how many, and how much flute each one uses, and ghosts the earlier passes into the section drawing.

Cut them top down, in the order they come out named — pass 1 of 3, then 2, then 3. That order isn't a preference. Every pass but the last has the tool sitting out over the part, and what keeps its flute clear is the material the pass above already took off. Run them backwards and you're burying the bit again, which is the whole reason the extra passes are there.

You may see faint lines across the face where the passes meet. They sand out. A bigger bit does the lot in one pass with none, so if you have one, use it.

Sharp corners

Corners that come to a point

A chamfer's corners normally come out rounded. At one depth the bit can only get so far into a corner, so it leaves a radius behind. Tick Sharp corners and the bit rises as it drives into each corner, and the two edges meet in a crisp point.

Raised lettering is where you notice it. Select the outlines and their counters together, leave Side on Auto, tick the box, and it works out which way each shape goes on its own — outward round the outlines, inward into the counters.

There's a limit to how deep the machine will sharpen. Past it, EdgeBreaker hands the cut over to Aspire's own chamfer engine, which runs the tip of the bit down the corner line and keeps the corners sharp at any size. The trade is that you give up the cut position buttons for that chamfer, because the cut has to come off the tip. The dialog greys them out and says so.

One window

Everything on one screen

The tool picker is in the header, the size and start depth sit under the drawing, and which edges get chamfered runs down the right-hand side. The middle is the cut you're about to make, and it isn't only a picture — drag the orange dot to set the chamfer size, the yellow square to set the start depth, the blue dot on the bit to pick where on the flute it cuts. Beside it, the preview paints the band the chamfer takes onto your own artwork, so a thin stroke that's about to lose its flat top shows up before you cut it.

The selection decides

It reads what you've selected before it opens

You shouldn't have to tell it what you're doing when it can see for itself. Before the dialog opens, EdgeBreaker looks at your selection and sets itself up to do the obvious thing. A coloured banner names it, and the OK button repeats it — so a run that would destroy something says so before you press anything.

You're never stuck with its guess — a dropdown in the banner picks any chamfer in the job, and the banner re-colours to match.

Each chamfer remembers the shapes it was built from and the settings that built them, stored inside the job file. Save the job and it's all still there when you open it again, on any machine.

Up to 99 per job

Different chamfers in the same job

Each chamfer gets its own bit, size and cut position, its own layer and its own toolpath. Rebuilding one leaves every other one exactly as it was.

The cutters come from your own tool database — angle, diameter, feeds, speeds and tool number, used exactly as you have them. There's nothing to set up inside the gadget, and you change your numbers in one place like always.

Edges below the top

Pockets and second levels

If the edge sits down in a pocket, or on a face you've already cut away, tell it how far down the surface is and the whole cut moves down to meet it. The chamfer itself doesn't change — the bit against the edge is the same relationship wherever that edge happens to sit.

When it works, it says nothing

No dialog to dismiss

A clean run shows you nothing at all. The new toolpath in the list and the orange offset vectors on the canvas are the confirmation — you don't need a window telling you what you just typed.

You only hear from it when there's something worth acting on: a shape too narrow to chamfer at the size you asked for, a vector it skipped, a remembered shape that's gone, or a toolpath the software wouldn't build.

Get it

It's free

I built and tested it in Aspire 12, and someone's now run it in VCarve Pro 12.5. There's nothing in it that needs either one in particular — it draws vectors, offsets them and makes a Profile toolpath — so older versions should be fine too. I just haven't tried them. Tell me how you get on.

Download EdgeBreaker.vgadget Project page & source
  1. Double-click the file to install, then restart Aspire or VCarve.
  2. Open a job and select the closed vectors you want to chamfer.
  3. Gadgets menu → EdgeBreaker. Pick your bit, type a size, choose a cut position, press OK.
  4. Not what you wanted? Run it again — your vectors are still selected, so it opens on that chamfer with the settings you used.

Already have it? Same steps — it installs over the old copy, and your existing chamfers and settings are untouched. The gadget doesn't check for updates, and the version it's on is printed at the top of the dialog.

Windows might tell you it doesn't recognise the file. That's only because it's new and not many people have downloaded it yet — choose Keep, then open it. There's a Help button in the dialog that opens the full guide, and it works with no internet.
